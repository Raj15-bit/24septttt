01

Money got dear everywhere at once, and the weakest links broke first

Long-term borrowing costs jumped to 20-year highs, and 2 very different things cracked on the same day.

In short
  • Lending money to the US government for 10 years now pays more than 5% a year, the most since 2007.
  • A giant AI data centre that depends on cheap loans sent a legal warning that it may be late.
  • In India, a regulator's plan to cut insurance commissions wiped out about a third of 1 company's value in a day.

Borrowing for a long time now costs what it cost before 2008

A bond yield is the yearly return a lender demands for handing money to a borrower for a fixed time. When the yield on long government bonds rises, every other long loan in the economy, from home loans to company bonds, gets dearer too. That is why this number sits under almost everything else in this issue.

The US 10-year yield crossed 5.1%, a level last seen before the 2008 crisis, and the 30-year yield reached its highest since June 2004. Japan and Europe moved the same way, so there was no cheap corner of the world to borrow in.

4 things pushed the numbers up together. The US economy is running hot, oil stays above $100 a barrel, the Federal Reserve raised its rate in mid-September, and buyers stepped back at government bond sales. Section 03 goes through each one.

The first crack: a data centre built on borrowed money

Oracle sent a force majeure notice on Project Jupiter, its huge data centre campus in New Mexico. A force majeure notice is a legal warning that events outside a company's control may stop it keeping a promise on time. Oracle says the project is still on schedule and that such notices are common, but lenders did not wait to find out.

  • Size — 4 data centres on about 1,400 acres, meant to sell about $300bn of computing to OpenAI.
  • Power — fuel cells from Bloom Energy, up to 2.45 GW; the gas pipeline feeding it is delayed to about February 2027.
  • The loan — an $18bn bank loan led by SMBC, BNP Paribas, Goldman Sachs and MUFG is quoted at 89–91 cents per dollar, and banks stopped selling it on to others.
  • Shares — Oracle fell about 5%, Bloom Energy about 4.4% and Blue Owl Capital, whose funds own the builder, about 2.3%.
  • Timing — the campus was due in November 2026 and is now talked about for 2028.

The same fear spread to other AI buildings. The bond behind Meta's Hyperion data centre, the largest company bond ever sold, fell to a record low near 93 cents. Oracle's bond due in 2056 now yields about 8%, more than the average junk bond, even though Oracle is still rated investment grade. So investors now charge AI builders like risky borrowers.

The second crack: a business built on high commissions

On the same day in India, the insurance regulator IRDAI published a draft plan to cap what insurers pay the people and companies that sell their policies. PB Fintech, which runs Policybazaar, fell 32% by the close and erased more than ₹28,000 crore of value.

Section 05 explains the plan in full. The point here is the pattern: a business earning a large fee for standing between a buyer and a seller is worth less when the rule-maker decides the fee is too high.

Why these 2 events are 1 story

Both depended on something cheap. Data centres needed cheap loans; insurance selling needed high, loosely checked commissions. When money gets dear, the value of profits that are far in the future shrinks the most, which is why expensive growth shares and debt-funded projects fall first.

Indian shares felt all of it at once. The Nifty 50 (the index of India's largest listed companies) has fallen for 7 weeks in a row, and it is heading for its worst September in 8 years.

02

Hard limits: ships, pipelines and power plants cannot be printed

The things that are physically short right now: safe sea lanes, oil in storage, and electricity for both steel mills and AI.

In short
  • Ships near the Strait of Hormuz are still being hit, and 1 Indian sailor died.
  • India's oil companies lose about ₹530 crore a day selling fuel below cost.
  • Power, not chips, is now the main thing slowing new AI data centres in the US.

The sea lane is still dangerous

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow channel between Iran and Oman that Gulf oil tankers must pass through. The war between Iran and the US and Israel began in February 2026, and ships in and near the strait are still being attacked.

  • MV Cape Dao — a bulk carrier sailing to India was hit by 2 torpedoes off Musandam, Oman. Of 28 crew, 20 were Indian; wiper Suraj Yadav from Deoria, Uttar Pradesh, was killed.
  • Another cargo ship — struck by an unknown projectile in the strait, caught fire and drifted; 2 casualties were reported.
  • Sailors killed — at least 23 since the war began. A seafarers' union asked the Prime Minister for Indian naval escorts.
  • Iran's terms — Iran wants the strait opened only under its own control and along a route agreed with Oman; the US wants free passage.

Saudi Arabia built a pipeline in the 1980s to avoid this problem. The East-West pipeline carries crude across the country to the Red Sea. Drones damaged it this month, and it is expected to restart at only half its size.

  • Aramco — chief executive Amin Nasser says breaks can be fixed "within days", but the company is studying a 4th and 5th export route, and storage abroad, including in Japan.
  • Sales — Saudi Arabia still sold almost 100 million barrels to buyers in China, India, Japan and South Korea in about a week.
  • Exports — shipping data from Kpler shows Saudi exports fell from about 7 million barrels a day in January to about 2.5 million in early September before recovering.

Tankers are at record rates and the US emergency reserve is nearly empty

A VLCC (very large crude carrier) holds about 2 million barrels. The daily hire cost for such tankers on Gulf routes to China, India and Japan is close to $1 million a day, far above the spikes of 2019 and 2020.

The US keeps a Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR), oil stored for emergencies like this one. It fell by another 405,000 barrels to 285 million, the lowest since 1982. So the US has less spare oil to release if prices jump again.

  • US crude stocks — rose by 2.97 million barrels against an expected fall, because refiners bought less.
  • Who used reserves — data from the International Energy Agency for January to July shows North America released the most; Europe released very little and holds its reserves mostly as diesel.

India buys about 89% of the crude oil it uses from abroad. That share has risen over 4 years while home output fell, so a higher oil price hits India harder than most large economies.

For AI, electricity is now the bottleneck

Data centres need huge, steady amounts of power, and the grid in many US states cannot connect them fast enough. Governments and grid operators are starting to push back.

  • Texas — Governor Greg Abbott ordered the state environment agency to halt all new data-centre permits until the grid operator ERCOT finishes an audit. "Data centers must pay their own way," he said.
  • Illinois — the utility ComEd tried to cancel a grid deal for a $20bn, 1.8 GW data centre in Joliet; the developer had posted just $1 of credit support.
  • Turbines — large gas turbines are sold out through 2030, and GE Vernova turbines are queued for 2030–31.
  • Own power — Goldman Sachs expects about 67 GW of on-site ("behind-the-meter") power at data centres by 2030; Vertiv says operators already choose on-site generation.
  • Korea — the first project under South Korea's $350bn investment pledge to the US is a $22.3bn, 6.3 GW gas plant in Encinal, Texas, costing about $3,540 per kW, with no buyer contracts signed yet.
  • Nuclear — Alphabet's Google will pay to squeeze about 96 MW more out of 2 Georgia nuclear plants run by Southern Co.
  • Gas pipes — TC Energy's Mountaineer XPress line in West Virginia declared force majeure; US gas futures rose 4.5% to $3.16.

Goldman also warns that the sudden swings in power use between busy and idle AI chips can cause local blackouts if servers are plugged straight into the grid. So a data centre now needs its own power plant, batteries and grid approval, and each adds time and cost.

In India, state power plants sit idle while private ones run full

Punjab promised farmers 8 hours of free power a day in the paddy season, but they got only a few. Its 3 state-owned coal plants had more coal than they needed, yet ran at under half their capacity.

Money and rates: a hot economy, a rising Fed and fewer buyers for US debt

Why long-term interest rates jumped, who stopped buying, and how it reaches home loans, the rupee and Indian bonds.

In short
  • The US economy is growing fast while oil keeps costs high, so the Fed started raising rates again.
  • Foreign buyers, who once bought US bonds every month, are now buying far less.
  • The rupee is near a record low of about 96 to the dollar, and Indian bond yields are rising too.

The US economy is running hot

A PMI (purchasing managers' index) is a monthly survey of company bosses; a reading above 50 means business is growing. September's early US readings came in far above forecasts, and firms said their own costs rose at the fastest pace in 4 years because of fuel and freight.

Where US households and companies feel it

The US government now pays about 3.6% of GDP in net interest, while companies pay only about 0.4%, because many firms locked in low rates during the pandemic and the government did not. So higher rates hurt the budget and home buyers first, and big companies later.

  • Home loans — 30-year mortgage rates reached 7.26%, the highest since mid-2024; more buyers are taking riskier loans.
  • House sellers — sellers outnumber buyers by 57.9%, a record; nearly 1 in 5 homes had a price cut in August.
  • New homes — sales rose to 684,000 against 616,000 expected, but the average price fell by a record $47,700 to $478,700.
  • Offices — the share of office loans in commercial property bonds that are behind on payments is about 12%, above the 2008 peak.
  • Shares — more than half of S&P 500 companies trade below their 200-day average; new 52-week lows beat new highs for 7 days in a row.
  • Small companies — the Russell 2000 fund IWM lost $3.3bn in a week of withdrawals.
  • Money supply — M2 (cash plus bank deposits) rose by $124.9bn in August to a record $23.34 trillion.
  • Spending — Bank of America card spending was up 6.9% from a year ago, but costly petrol is opening a gap between richer and poorer households.

India: the rupee near 96 and bond yields rising

When US bonds pay over 5%, the gap to Indian bonds shrinks, so foreign investors have less reason to hold rupee assets. The rupee is near its record low of about 96 per dollar, even with the Reserve Bank of India selling dollars to steady it.

  • Indian bonds — the 10-year yield rose 5 bp to 7.09%, a 4-month high.
  • Cash market — on 23 September foreign investors still bought ₹1,617 crore of shares and domestic funds ₹2,342 crore.
  • Futures — foreign investors' index futures positions were 89% bets on a fall.

Yet India's growth outlook went up, not down. After GDP grew 7.8% in April to June, with investment spending up 11.9%, several agencies raised their forecasts for this financial year above the RBI's own.

Commodities and trade: crude swings on every headline, but diesel is the real shock

Where the headline oil price misleads, who is buying what, and how new US rules on medicines and Russian oil reach India.

In short
  • Brent crude moved between about $101 and $107 a barrel on news from Iran.
  • Diesel has risen twice as fast as crude this year, and the US may ban diesel exports.
  • India won a 0% US tariff on special medicines but faces a new US law aimed at buyers of Russian oil.

Brent follows the Iran news, hour by hour

Brent is the main world benchmark price for crude oil. It rose to about $106 when an adviser to Iran's Supreme Leader, Yahya Rahim Safavi, said the war could spread "to the Indian Ocean and perhaps beyond" if the US attacks again. It then fell when reports said a deal was in motion, and rose again when an Iranian official called those reports false.

  • The talks — US and Iranian teams are exploring a step-by-step deal: Iran reopens Hormuz, the US eases its blockade. Gulf states oppose Iran keeping "administrative control" of the strait.
  • Iran's reach — Iran fired missiles at the US-UK base on Diego Garcia in March, and has threatened a second front at Bab el-Mandeb, the entrance to the Red Sea.
  • US view — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says Hormuz will be "worthless" within 2 years as other routes are built; Qatar's energy minister disagrees.

Diesel has its own crisis

Refiners turn crude into petrol, diesel and jet fuel. The "crack spread" is the gap between the price of a fuel and the price of the crude it is made from. On the US Gulf Coast the diesel crack passed $100 a barrel in August for the first time ever; the normal range is $15 to $30.

Because trucks, farms and ships run on diesel, a diesel shock spreads into the price of almost everything. The fund manager Apollo calls it a "core inflation shock" and says diesel margins now set the long end of the US bond market.

  • Export ban — Politico reported the White House is preparing a 90-day ban on US diesel exports to cut prices before the November elections; Energy Secretary Chris Wright had said a ban "would not work".
  • Market reaction — US diesel futures fell more than 7% and European diesel futures rose more than 7% within minutes.
  • Industry letter — the whole US oil industry, the US Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Manufacturers wrote to President Trump against any limit, which is very unusual.
  • Who ships diesel — the Middle East, Russia and the US together supply about 80% of diesel traded by sea, so a US ban would squeeze Europe most; the EU meets on it on 29 September.

Another way to read the ban talk is that it is pressure on Europe and the UK to release diesel from their own emergency stocks. Either way, diesel stays the tightest part of the oil market, and India's state refiners take the loss at the pump, as section 02 showed.

China is buying less oil, and from different places

China's crude imports by sea are about 7.2 million barrels a day, down 2.7 million (27%) from a year earlier, according to Kpler and Goldman Sachs. It now buys less from Russia and Iran and more on the open market, because Hormuz is blocked and Venezuelan oil no longer goes to China.

So the Chinese "buying spree" is pickier, not hungrier. Shanghai crude futures trade above $100, higher than Brent, which shows how tight supply is inside China.

Food: wheat and edible oil

Attacks in the Black Sea halved grain exports from Russia and Ukraine to about 8 million tonnes for July to September. Bangladesh has turned back to Indian wheat for the first time since India banned exports in May 2022; India lifted the ban in late August.

  • Bangladesh — more than 200,000 tonnes booked at $305–326 a tonne delivered. Before the ban, India supplied about 70% of Bangladesh's wheat.
  • World prices — wheat futures are up about 40% from June lows; flour in Dhaka rose 17% in a month.

Metals: copper, tungsten and aluminium

  • Copper — BHP closed the world's largest copper mine, in Chile, after a worker died, with no restart date. That draws attention to Hindustan Copper, India's only integrated copper miner.
  • Tungsten — prices are up about 8 times since early 2025 and China controls about 80% of supply. Goldman Sachs started coverage of Almonty Industries, owner of the Sangdong mine in South Korea, with a $13 target, far below others; the shares fell 6.5%.
  • Aluminium — India's trade remedies body DGTR recommended extending anti-dumping duties for 5 years on aluminium sheets and foil from China, Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia. The current duty expires on 5 December 2026. This protects makers such as Hindalco.

India and the new US law on Russian oil buyers

President Trump signed the Sanctioning Russia Act, a law pushed by Senator Lindsey Graham that allows tariffs of 100% or more on countries that buy Russian oil. External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar raised it with Secretary of State Marco Rubio in New York, and India is signalling that its patience is "strategic", not endless.

  • India's buying — imports of Russian crude fell 16.5% in August from July, to about 2.1 million barrels a day; total crude imports fell 8.8% to 4.44 million, with more from Iraq and Saudi Arabia.
  • Visits — Rubio is due in India on 19 October, and Prime Minister Modi is expected to visit the US, Canada and Europe in December.
  • Europe — the India-EU free trade agreement is set to be signed on 16 December; Germany wants to sign a deal of about $8bn for 6 submarines, built with Mazagon Dock and Thyssenkrupp Marine Systems.
  • Corridor — President Trump backed IMEC, the India-Middle East-Europe trade corridor announced in Delhi in 2023, as a way to move goods away from "Iranian checkpoints".
  • UN — India, Brazil, Germany and Japan (the G4) again pushed for permanent seats on the UN Security Council.

India, policy against price: the insurance regulator resets who gets paid

What IRDAI proposed, why it acted, who loses most, and the other rule changes from SEBI, NPCI and the government.

In short
  • IRDAI wants to cap what insurers pay banks, agents and websites for selling policies.
  • Payouts to sellers grew about 4 times faster than the premiums customers paid.
  • Lenders that earn a large part of their profit from selling insurance, like IndusInd Bank, are most exposed.

What the regulator proposed

The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) released a draft titled "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution". It is a consultation paper, not a final rule, and comments are open until 25 October 2026.

"Expenses of management" (EoM) are everything an insurer spends to run its business and sell policies, shown as a share of premium. The paper would cut the allowed share for both life and general insurers, and set separate caps on commissions for each product.

Why the regulator acted

Over the 4 years to FY25, the commission paid to sellers grew more than twice as fast as the premium customers paid. Some payments were even labelled as advertising or marketing to get around the limits.

PB Fintech: a fall in steps, and a put option that rose 100 times

Shares in the futures and options (F&O) segment have "flexible" price bands. Instead of 1 daily limit, trading pauses at each step down, and the limit widens after a cooling-off break. That is why PB Fintech fell in a staircase through the day.

  • Price bands — 10%, then 15%, 20%, 23%, 26%, 28%, 30%, 32% and 34%, each after a pause of 15 to 60 minutes.
  • Path — ₹1,886 → ₹1,698 → ₹1,603 → ₹1,509 → ₹1,452 → ₹1,396 → ₹1,282 at the close.
  • Size of fall — about 32% at the close and up to 36% at the low, its biggest single-day fall since listing.
  • Put option — the September 1,800 put (a contract that pays when a share falls below a price) went from ₹3.70 to about ₹510.
  • Info Edge — owns about 6.5% of PB Fintech and lost about ₹1,700 crore of value in a day.
  • Turtlemint — the listed insurance marketplace fell 20% to its lower limit; a company insider had sold 10,120 shares the day before.
  • Buyer — HDFC Mutual Fund bought 25 lakh shares at ₹1,282.30, about ₹320.6 crore, in a block deal.
  • Income — PB's insurance income was about ₹6,100 crore in FY26, up 42%.

Words explained

Every term used above, in 1 sentence each.

52-week high or lowThe highest or lowest price a share has traded at over the past year.
ADC (antibody-drug conjugate)A cancer medicine that joins an antibody to a strong drug so the drug goes mainly to cancer cells.
AGR duesOld licence and spectrum fees that Indian telecom companies were ordered to pay on a wider definition of revenue.
Anchor investorA large investor who agrees to buy part of a share sale a day before it opens to the public.
Anti-dumping dutyAn extra import tax on goods sold in India below their fair price in the home country.
Auction tailThe extra yield a government must pay at a bond sale above what traders expected just before it; a big tail means weak demand.
BancassuranceBanks selling insurance policies to their own customers in return for a commission.
BarrelAbout 159 litres of crude oil; oil prices are quoted per barrel.
Basis point (bp)One-hundredth of a percentage point; 25 basis points is 0.25%.
Behind-the-meter powerElectricity made on site, for example at a data centre, rather than taken from the public grid.
Bid-to-cover ratioThe value of bids at a bond sale divided by the value of bonds sold; lower means less demand.
BilletA semi-finished bar of steel that is later rolled into products like TMT bars.
Block dealA single large trade of shares between 2 parties at 1 agreed price, done in a special exchange window.
BrentThe main world benchmark price for crude oil, based on North Sea oil.
Bulk dealA trade in which 1 party buys or sells more than 0.5% of a company's shares in a day.
CDMOContract development and manufacturing organisation: a company that makes drug ingredients for the drug's owner.
Circuit limitThe largest rise or fall a share may make in a day before trading in it stops.
Commission capA legal limit on how much a seller can be paid, as a share of the premium, for selling a policy.
Consultation paperA draft rule a regulator publishes to collect comments before deciding the final rule.
Crack spreadThe gap between the price of a refined fuel, such as diesel, and the crude oil it is made from.
Credit default swap (CDS)A contract that pays out if a borrower fails to repay; its price rises when lenders get more worried.
CRISPRA system found in bacteria that can cut and edit DNA; it is now the base of gene-editing medicines.
Dark patternsWebsite or app designs that trick users into choices they did not mean to make.
Data centreA building full of computer servers that store data and run software, including AI models.
DerivativeA contract whose value depends on the price of something else, such as a share, index or commodity.
DGTRDirectorate General of Trade Remedies, the Indian body that investigates unfair imports and suggests duties.
DistributorAnyone who sells a product on behalf of its maker, such as a bank or agent selling insurance.
DRAMThe main working memory in computers and servers; cheaper and slower than HBM.
EBITDAProfit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; a rough measure of operating profit.
Expenses of management (EoM)All the costs an insurer spends to run its business and sell policies, as a share of premium.
F&O segmentThe futures and options part of the stock market, where derivatives on shares and indices trade.
FabA factory that makes the silicon chips themselves.
Fiscal dominanceWhen a government's debts are so large that the central bank's rate decisions are driven by the need to keep the debt affordable.
Flash PMIAn early estimate of a monthly business survey, released before the final figure.
Force majeureA legal notice that events outside a company's control may stop it keeping a promise on time.
FPIForeign portfolio investor: a foreign fund that buys Indian shares, bonds or derivatives.
GCC (global capability centre)An office in India that a foreign company owns and uses for its own technology, finance or engineering work.
GDPGross domestic product: the total value of goods and services an economy produces in a year.
Generic medicineA copy of a drug whose patent has expired, usually sold far cheaper than the original.
GPUGraphics processing unit: the chip most used to train and run AI models.
Grey market premiumThe unofficial price gain traders expect on a new share before it lists.
GW (gigawatt)1,000 megawatts of power; roughly the output of 1 large nuclear reactor.
HBMHigh-bandwidth memory: very fast, costly memory stacked on AI chips.
HVDCHigh-voltage direct current: a way to send large amounts of power over long distances with low losses.
HyperscalerOne of the very large cloud companies, such as Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta.
IMECThe India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor, a planned trade route by ship, rail and road.
Import dependenceThe share of a country's use of something, such as crude oil, that it buys from abroad.
Index futuresContracts to buy or sell a share index, such as the Nifty, at a set price on a future date.
Indirect biddersBuyers at US bond sales who bid through dealers, mostly foreign central banks and big funds.
Investment gradeA credit rating that says a borrower is fairly safe; below it, bonds are called junk.
IPOInitial public offering: a company's first sale of shares to the public before listing.
IRDAIThe Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, which regulates insurers and their sellers.
Junk bondA bond from a borrower rated below investment grade, which must pay higher interest.
LFP batteryLithium iron phosphate battery: cheaper and safer than other lithium types, now dominant in China.
Load factorThe share of an airline's seats that are sold.
M2A measure of money in an economy: cash plus most bank deposits.
MDRMerchant discount rate: the fee a shop pays for accepting a digital payment.
Mis-sellingSelling a customer a product that does not suit them, often by hiding costs or tying it to a loan.
NBFCNon-bank finance company: a lender that is not a bank, such as Bajaj Finance.
NEVNew energy vehicle: China's term for battery-electric and plug-in hybrid cars.
Nifty 50The index of India's 50 largest companies listed on NSE.
OMCOil marketing company: in India, the state firms that sell petrol, diesel and cooking gas.
Order bookThe value of orders a company has won but not yet delivered.
OSATOutsourced semiconductor assembly and test: a plant that packages and tests chips made elsewhere.
P/E ratioPrice-to-earnings ratio: the share price divided by yearly profit per share.
PLI schemeProduction-linked incentive: government payments to factories for each extra unit they make in India.
PMIPurchasing managers' index: a monthly survey of businesses; above 50 means activity is growing.
PMSPortfolio management service: a manager who runs a personal share portfolio for a wealthy client.
Policy rateThe main interest rate a central bank sets, which guides all other rates.
Preferential issueNew shares or warrants sold to chosen buyers at a set price, rather than to the public.
PremiumThe price a customer pays for an insurance policy.
Put optionA contract that gains value when a share falls below a set price.
QIPQualified institutional placement: a quick sale of new shares by a listed company to large institutions.
Refurbished phoneA used phone that has been checked, repaired and resold with a warranty.
Renewal commissionThe commission paid to a seller each year a customer keeps paying for a policy.
RFPRequest for proposal: a formal request from a buyer asking suppliers to offer terms for a job.
Rights issueNew shares offered to existing shareholders in proportion to what they already own.
SME platformA separate stock exchange platform for small companies, with lighter listing rules.
SPRStrategic Petroleum Reserve: the oil the US government stores for emergencies.
SPVSpecial purpose vehicle: a separate company set up to hold 1 project and its debts.
Strait of HormuzThe narrow sea channel between Iran and Oman that Gulf oil tankers must pass through.
Synchronous condenserA large spinning machine that steadies a power grid with lots of solar and wind power.
Take rateThe share of each sale that a platform keeps as its fee.
TokenA small piece of text, about three-quarters of a word, that AI models read and write and are priced by.
